The problem

Your best guests are the most valuable fraud targets in the building. A text that says "this is your host — send your deposit here" costs an attacker nothing and can cost a VIP relationship everything. Phone numbers get spoofed, SMS gets phished, and personal texting between hosts and guests leaves the casino with no record and no control.

Today there is no channel where a guest can be certain the person messaging them is really their host.

How it works

01 — On property

The ceremony

When a guest joins the program, their host taps a physical security key on the guest's phone — in person, at the casino. That thirty-second moment creates a cryptographic bond that cannot be faked, phished, or reset remotely.

02 — Everywhere after

The channel

From then on, every message is end-to-end encrypted and signed by that key. If a message appears in the Seal channel, it came from your host. Anything arriving anywhere else, the guest knows to ignore.

03 — For the house

The record

Unlike hosts texting from personal phones, the property holds an audit copy of every conversation — supporting Title 31 and gaming-commission expectations instead of working around them.

Why the inconvenience is the product

Every other messaging app is easy to join — which is exactly why fraud is easy. Seal is deliberately hard to join: identity lives on a physical key, and connections only form face to face. For a consumer app that's a flaw. For a VIP program it's a feature that doubles as hospitality — a brass-key enrollment ceremony that tells your best guests they've entered something exclusive, and tells fraudsters the door is closed.

A conversation with player development and the gaming commission — not a sale. We want to learn whether host–guest impersonation and off-channel texting are problems Spirit Mountain feels today, and what a compliant pilot with five hosts and twenty invited guests would need to look like.
