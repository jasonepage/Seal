How Seal works, in full

Written for somebody who already owns a hardware wallet and has been trained, correctly, to distrust anything that asks for a seed phrase. Every claim here points at a file you can read.

The honest advice first. If you own a hardware wallet, do not put a live seed phrase into any beta software, including this one. Nobody outside this project has audited Seal. What your family cannot reconstruct without you is where the steel plate is, which wallet it belongs to, and who to call first, and none of that needs the twelve words. Start there.

Read the code first

Six files, in the order SECURITY.md puts them. Between them they decide who can unwrap what, what one key holder learns alone, when a release is allowed, and whether a key was really tapped.

Seal/Estate/EstateKeys.swiftEvery wrap and unwrap in the estate.
Seal/Crypto/KEMBundle.swiftX25519 plus ML-KEM-768.
Seal/Crypto/Shamir.swiftThe key split, over GF(256).
Seal/Estate/ReleaseMachine.swiftThe countdown, as a pure function.
Seal/Crypto/WebAuthnParsing.swiftAssertion checks: relying party hash, user presence, type.
tools/verify_capsule.pyThe standalone verifier.

docs/PRE_AUDIT.md is written for a reviewer: where to attack in order, and the weak spots already accepted. It ends with the commands that reproduce the key split, a synthetic capsule and its verification on any machine, with no Mac and no phone. Those same commands run on every push.

The primitives, named

All from Apple’s CryptoKit and AuthenticationServices. No third party cryptography and no external dependencies at all.

IdentityWebAuthn credential, P-256 ECDSA with SHA-256. A hardware security key or a platform passkey. The private key never leaves the authenticator.
Per device keyP-256 in the Secure Enclave, non-exportable, endorsed by a signature from the identity credential.
WrappingHybrid: X25519 ECDH plus ML-KEM-768. The two shared secrets are combined through HKDF-SHA256. ML-KEM needs iOS 26, so a wrap to a device below that is the classical half alone. The wrap records which suite it used, so a reader can tell which protection a given share actually has.
ContentAES-256-GCM, with a domain separated AAD naming the estate, the epoch and the purpose, so a ciphertext made for one slot cannot be replayed into another.
The key splitShamir’s Secret Sharing over GF(256), M of N. Checked against independently generated Python test vectors.
TimeRFC 3161 timestamp tokens on the events that drive the countdown: check-ins, cancellations, claims, key taps, releases, a departure, and each time an epoch is published. Only the event’s digest leaves the phone. Other record entries, such as meeting somebody in person, stay unstamped until you turn that on.
StorageApple CloudKit public database, holding ciphertext and public keys. There is no Seal server.

The key hierarchy

Envelope Content Key   random 256 bit, AES-256-GCM, one per envelope
    listed in
Key Table              one per RECIPIENT, under its own random Key Table Key
    reachable two ways
    ├─ wrapped to the OWNER’s devices
    └─ encrypted under the Estate Key, then wrapped to the RECIPIENT’s devices
Estate Key             one per rule per epoch
    reachable two ways
    ├─ wrapped to the OWNER’s devices
    └─ split by Shamir into N shares, threshold M, each wrapped to one
       key holder’s devices

The same thing in the usual notation, for people who read that faster:

c_env      = Enc(m, k_env)                            one random k_env per envelope
Table_r    = Enc({k_env, ...}, k_table_r)             one table per recipient r
k_table_r  reachable two ways:
             Wrap(k_table_r, pk_owner)
             Wrap(Enc(k_table_r, K_estate), pk_r)     needs recipient’s phone AND K_estate
K_estate   = Shamir split into s_1 .. s_N, threshold M
s_i        = Wrap(s_i, pk_holder_i)                   one piece per key holder

Wrap is X25519 plus ML-KEM-768 through HKDF-SHA256. Enc is AES-256-GCM with the estate, epoch and purpose in the AAD. The same diagram is at the top of EstateKeys.swift, where the code still uses the older word custodian for a key holder.

Read it from the bottom. To open one envelope you need its content key. That is in a key table. That table is locked under a key reachable either by the owner’s own phone, or by the Estate Key plus the recipient’s own phone. The Estate Key is reachable either by the owner’s phone, or by combining M of N Shamir shares, each locked to one key holder’s phone.

An envelope picks a rule, and envelopes that share a rule share one Estate Key. Each rule is its own box, with its own key holders, its own shares, its own claim and its own taps, so releasing the urgent envelopes reveals nothing about the rest.

The consequence people miss

The people holding your keys, acting together, recover the Estate Key and nothing else. The Estate Key alone opens no envelope. Each key table still needs the phone of the person that table was written for. So the two key holders who release your estate cannot read your wife’s letter, or your children’s, or each other’s. They hold the inner key of every table and the outer key of none of them.

What each person actually holds

Everybody who takes part holds two separate secrets, not one.

Their credentialThe passkey or security key they registered with. It signs “I authorise this claim”, bound to the estate, the epoch, the claim and the record’s current head, so the signature means that one claim and nothing else.
Their phoneA separate key pair held in that phone’s keychain, marked this-device-only. It does not sync to iCloud and does not restore onto a new phone. This is what opens their Shamir share.

Both are required. A passkey syncs through iCloud, so somebody who takes over a key holder’s Apple account can produce the signature. They cannot produce the share, because the share can only be opened on the physical phone it was wrapped to. In practice the bar is M unlocked phones, in M different people’s hands.

The direct consequence, which we would rather you read here than discover in a decade: a key holder who replaces their phone loses their share until the owner seals again. The owner’s app is meant to notice and say so.

Why people meet in person

Every remote identity system eventually trusts a channel: a phone number, an email address, a recovery code, a support agent. Each of those is a thing a stranger can take from you without being anywhere near you.

Seal trusts one event instead. Two people stand together, and each phone records the other’s public key in that moment. From then on, a directory that later serves a different key under the same name is refused on both phones, every fetch, permanently. Key holders learn each other’s keys the same way, from the owner’s signed statement, so the directory cannot insert a stranger between them either.

The countdown, precisely

  • Opening the app writes one signed line saying you were here. That is the heartbeat, and it needs your phone and at most Face ID or Touch ID. It never needs the hardware key, because a living person who lost their key must not be declared dead by their own software.
  • After the silence you chose, ninety days by default, a key holder may start a claim. A claim posted before the silence has fully elapsed is ignored, not honoured with a shorter countdown.
  • Every phone also records the day it first saw each claim and each key tap, and counts from that day if the signed time is older. So a key holder whose phone claims the request was made forty days ago cannot skip the warnings.
  • A claim starts weeks of daily warnings, then a further quiet period. Any key holder may object, which pauses or kills the claim depending on your rule.
  • One tap from you at any point cancels everything, and every key holder sees that it was cancelled and by whom.
  • Only after all of it can keys be tapped. Each tap re-wraps that person’s share to whoever started the claim, and the claimant’s phone combines them.

The machine that decides all of this is a pure function of the record and the clock, with no network in it, in ReleaseMachine.swift.

The record, event by event

Every line in the record is signed by the device that wrote it and carries the digest of the newest line that device had seen. These are all the kinds there are, from docs/CAPSULE.md section 5.

KindWritten byWhat is in it
estateCreatedownerthe rule, and when
epochPublishedownerepoch, threshold, the key holders’ account IDs and public keys, one commitment per share, the Estate Key commitment, the digest of the material
policyChangedownerthe new rule
vaultUpdatedownera commitment to the blobs, and the table IDs
heartbeatownernothing. Its existence is the point
cancellationownerthe claim being stopped, or nothing
silenceObservedkey holderthe last heartbeat this phone saw, and when. Evidence only; it changes nothing
releaseClaimedkey holderclaim ID, epoch, the last heartbeat time, and a reason they typed
objectionkey holderthe claim, and a note they typed
objectionWithdrawnkey holderthe claim, and a note
authorizationkey holderclaim ID, epoch, the record head, the WebAuthn assertion from their key, and their share re-wrapped to the claimant
releasedkey holderclaim ID, epoch, which share indexes were used, and the Estate Key in the clear
custodyConfirmedkey holderepoch, the record head, and an assertion. The yearly “I still have my key”
ownerDepartedownerwhether to keep the envelopes for the family or cancel them, and when

An event by anybody else, or an owner event of a key holder kind or the reverse, is invalid and ignored. A custodyConfirmed is signed over a different domain string from an authorization, on purpose, so a yearly check-in cannot be counted as a release tap.

The capsule

Anybody holding part of an estate can export one JSON file carrying the whole signed history. It holds:

  • every public key involved: the owner, every key holder, every recipient who appears, and each of their endorsed device keys;
  • the full record of events in order, each signed and linked to the one before it, with the RFC 3161 tokens where they were obtained;
  • the epoch material: the Estate Key wrapped to the owner’s devices, and each key holder’s share wrapped to that key holder’s devices, with commitments;
  • the key tables, one per recipient, encrypted;
  • optionally the encrypted envelopes themselves.

Nothing in it is readable without the private keys held on people’s phones. One command checks it:

python3 tools/verify_capsule.py seal-capsule-XXXX.json --strict

What that command checks, one line at a time.

What somebody reading the stored database can see

The CloudKit public database is world readable, and any signed-in Apple account can write records into it. Every reader checks signatures and drops the rest, so writing junk costs a reader fetch time, not correctness. Reading is the interesting half:

What is thereWhat it gives
Your identity recordReadable: the display name you chose, your public keys, your account ID. Public on purpose, so people you meet can check it is you.
Your ruleReadable: silence days, warning days, the quiet period after them, the threshold, and what an objection does.
Your key holdersReadable as account IDs: who they are in Seal’s terms, how many there are, and how many it takes.
Every event, and whenReadable: check-ins, seals, claims, objections, key taps, releases, a departure. Signed, not encrypted.
A claim reason or an objection noteReadable as typed. A key holder writes those, and they are signed but not encrypted.
How many people you wrote toReadable: one key table per recipient, plus the number and rough size of the blobs.
Whether a given account has a part in some estateReadable: an invitation is addressed by a one way fingerprint of the person.
The Estate Key, after a releaseReadable. Publishing it in the clear is what a release is.
Titles, letters, secrets, photos, voice, video, filesClosed. AES-256-GCM under a content key that is itself inside an encrypted table.
Which recipient a key table belongs toClosed. A table names its recipient nowhere. Recipients find theirs by trying to open each one.
The Estate Key, before a releaseClosed. It exists only wrapped to the owner’s devices, and as shares wrapped to key holders’ devices.

None of the readable half is an accident, and none of it is fixable without a server that knows who is asking. It is written here, in docs/PRODUCT.md section 8, and on the privacy page, rather than left for somebody to find.

Rotation is cheap, on purpose

Removing somebody who holds a key means a new epoch: a fresh Estate Key, fresh shares, and the small inner ciphertext of each key table re-encrypted. The tables themselves and the media blobs are never touched, so changing your mind about a person costs the same whether you wrote two envelopes or two hundred. The old shares stop working the moment the new epoch is published.
